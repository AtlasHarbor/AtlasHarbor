OPERATIONS MANUAL

Run the company. Dispatch the exceptions.

You are the General Manager and Lead Dispatcher of a growing global 3PL. Departments handle routine work inside policy. You manage staffing, payroll, owned and leased equipment, contracted and partner-accessible capacity, carrier relationships, cash, customer promises, and international growth—then step into dispatch when judgment matters.

Your first morning

The first chapter starts with a U.S. truck breakdown so you can learn recovery decisions quickly. That incident is only one exception inside a worldwide network already moving through factories, ports, airports, distribution centers, trucks, ships, and aircraft.

Your role

  • General Manager: staffing, payroll, cash, policy, carrier relationships, fleet strategy, leases, capacity procurement, international expansion, and company trust.
  • Lead Dispatcher: difficult loads, driver coordination, exceptions, expedites, mode changes, threatened promises, and recovery.
  • 3PL network builder: decide what to own, what to lease, what to contract, which partner capacity to activate, and which lanes are worth pursuing.

The game is intentionally management by exception. If everything requires a click, the company is designed badly—but meaningful commitments should still require a deliberate decision.

Departments and staffing

Click the green routine-operations indicator or Manage departments to open the company console. Department cards show the active headcount, pending agency hires, department annual payroll, next-hire salary, staffing-agency fee, expected lead time, policy, and current workload.

  • Dispatch & Capacity — Diego Ramos: safe releases, capacity matching, driver availability, transport escalations.
  • Production Control — Nia Brooks: inventory readiness, packaging, quality, throughput, and handoffs.
  • Customer Operations — Maya Chen: routine updates, promises, relationship risk, and escalation.
  • Global Trade Desk — Amina Hassan: overseas partners, documents, customs readiness, and international handoffs.

Hiring is not instant

Request 1 hire opens a staffing-agency review. Atlas Harbor models a standard 20% placement fee on first-year salary, plus onboarding/equipment cost. The placement fee is paid when you approve the requisition, but the person does not count toward active headcount until the hiring lead time finishes.

The review shows current cash, upfront commitment, cash after approval, salary, annual people cost after the hire starts, lead time, current headcount, pending hires, and headcount after the start date. Approved hires remain in the staffing pipeline while background checks, notice period, and onboarding are simulated.

Reducing a position also requires confirmation and includes a severance cost. Policy and delegation changes are reviewed before they take effect.

Budget and payroll

The control tower shows cash alongside annual people budget, monthly people payroll, monthly asset commitments, and pending requisitions. Payroll and recurring lease/asset costs accrue against company cash as game time advances, so recurring overhead is not just a decorative number.

Fleet & capacity

Capacity is split into four different concepts:

  • Owned: equipment Atlas Harbor controls and maintains.
  • Leased: equipment Atlas Harbor controls during a lease, with recurring monthly cost.
  • Contracted/on hand: capacity already commercially committed and usable, such as truck blocks, ocean FEU, or air pallets.
  • Partner accessible: capacity a carrier says may be available. It is not yours until you contract it.

The Fleet & Capacity panel shows those four numbers at a glance for trucks, trailers, ocean FEU, and air pallets. This is intentionally different from a single undifferentiated “fleet count.”

The procurement catalog lets you review representative choices such as buying a tractor, leasing a tractor, leasing a trailer, contracting a truck flex block, contracting ocean FEU, or contracting air pallets. Every acquisition shows the upfront cost, monthly recurring cost where applicable, procurement lead time, current controlled capacity, partner-accessible capacity, and what controlled inventory will look like after delivery.

Carrier network

Carrier cards show markets, service reputation, relationship strength, commercial contact, and partner-accessible capacity by mode. Calling a carrier is an information/relationship action. Review capacity contract shows the amount you are converting from partner access into committed inventory, the cost, cash after approval, and expected availability before anything changes.

People & HR

Drivers have region, reliability, available hours, morale, status, and recurring annual labor cost. Driver candidates also come through a staffing-agency process with a 20% placement fee and lead time; they are not dispatchable immediately after approval.

Even a driver load offer opens a review showing hours, reliability, morale, estimated acceptance probability, and the resulting hours if accepted.

Confirmation before commitment

Major management actions use a shared Review Commitment surface. Staffing, position reductions, delegation changes, policy changes, maintenance, procurement, carrier contracts, driver hires, driver load offers, and lane bookings all require confirmation. Financial reviews show the current budget and the expected before/after state so you can make a management decision rather than discover the consequence after clicking.

Live global supply chains

The globe is not just opportunity dots. Routine supply chains visibly move while you work. Examples include electronics from Shenzhen through Yantian and across the Pacific to Southern California, EV parts from Busan to the Netherlands, healthcare packaging from Mumbai to Dubai, Kenyan coffee to the U.K., Japanese industrial components into Australia, Indonesian furniture into the Gulf, Colombian packaging into the U.S. Southeast, and Shanghai medical devices moving by priority air into Sydney.

Each flow has multiple legs. A shipment may display a 🚚 from factory to port, switch to 🚢 or ✈️ for international linehaul, then become a 🚚 again for destination distribution. Click a moving vehicle to inspect its customer, cargo, current leg, carrier, overall cycle progress, and current-leg progress.

Global network

The World Network includes the U.S., Colombia, Netherlands, U.K., UAE, India, Kenya, China, South Korea, Japan, Indonesia, and Australia. Fit global network returns to the worldwide view. Focus active chapter zooms back to the immediate U.S. exception.

Bookable international lanes

Candidate opportunities span China → U.S., Korea → Netherlands, UAE → India, Kenya → U.K., Japan → Australia, Indonesia → UAE, Colombia → U.S., and China → Australia air recovery. Lane booking is not instant: the review shows carrier, buy cost, cash after approval, expected revenue, gross spread before overhead, risk, and service time. Revenue is posted only when simulated service completes.

Routes and movement

U.S. truck routes use Atlas Harbor’s operating routing layer. Global traffic uses representative multimodal corridors: regional drayage stays on the relevant landmass, ocean services use curated maritime waypoints, and air movements use great-circle geometry. The international geometry is for simulation coherence, not live AIS, flight tracking, carrier schedules, customs routing, freight quotations, or navigation.

Game terms

Info icons explain terms including Management by exception, Challenge lane, Partner access, Lease, and Staffing agency. Challenge lane is a commercial action, not a physical reroute.

Time and persistence

The decision simulation advances in game hours. Staffing and capacity pipelines use game-hour availability dates, and recurring labor/lease economics accrue as game time advances. Game progress is saved on the device and, for signed-in users, inside user_metadata.atlas_problem_spaces.logistics_game.progress. Management state travels inside that same career object.

Reality boundary

Customer, carrier, employee, facility, salary, agency-fee, rate, reputation, and capacity values are fictional scenario inputs designed for gameplay. The 20% agency-fee mechanic follows the management assumption used by this simulation; it is not a claim that every real staffing agency or geography charges the same rate.
