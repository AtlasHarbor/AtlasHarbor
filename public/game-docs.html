How to run the Atlas Harbor logistics network

You are the operating manager of a third-party logistics company. Your job is to choose profitable contracts, protect service commitments, and decide how scarce trucks, drivers, cash, routes, and outside carriers should be used.

The objective

Complete as many client contracts as possible during a ten-day operating cycle while maintaining cash and client trust. Revenue is not profit: every shipment has handling, fuel, route, and provider costs.

The dispatch flow

  1. Select a contract. Review cargo, deadline, truck demand, risk, and revenue.
  2. Choose a route. Express is fast and expensive. Coastal is cheaper but more exposed. Intermodal is slower but resilient.
  3. Choose a provider. Your fleet protects margin but has limited trucks. Third-party carriers cost more but add capacity and reduce some risk.
  4. Dispatch. Cash is committed immediately. Trucks remain unavailable until delivery.
  5. Run the next day. Shipments progress, deliveries release resources, and disruptions may change plans.

Resources

Trucks and drivers are the main capacity constraint. Trailers support shipment volume. Cold-chain slots matter for refrigerated work. Cash funds fuel, handling, tolls, and carrier fees. Client trust rises with on-time delivery and falls when commitments are missed.

Routes and providers

The map starts with roads, a port lane, suppliers, a cross-dock, and client locations. You are not building arbitrary tiles. You are deciding how freight should move through an existing network.

How this translates

The game uses logistics as a concrete model of general strategy. Fleet capacity can represent roster spots or research attention. Routes can represent procedural choices. Third-party carriers can represent outside experts, replacement players, or alternative sources. Deadlines, congestion, dependencies, and disruption preserve their structural meaning across domains.

Design lineage

The game borrows proven conventions from transport-management games: visible moving vehicles, existing locations and infrastructure, explicit route assignment, cargo chains, vehicle capacity, operating economics, and escalating scenario objectives. Atlas Harbor adds a translation layer that explains what each operational choice may mean in another decision domain.
